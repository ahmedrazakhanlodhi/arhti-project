# Narrative

## Central question
Does arhti credit fill the gaps left by formal credit (H1), does formal credit reach small farms in proportion (H2), and is
lending recorded where farmland is (H3)?

## Main conclusion
Agricultural credit in Punjab has distinct and overlapping geographies. H1 is not supported: arhti credit clusters near grain markets,
and no formal source shows a significant negative association with it. H2 is not supported: small holdings appear in borrower records in proportion
but receive a much smaller share of disbursement. H3 is not supported: lending is recorded heavily in a few city districts.

## Introduction
Punjab's farmers borrow from banks and from arhtis, the commission agents who sell their crops in grain markets. We look at four
lenses: where credit is reported as available (access), who formal credit reaches (allocation), where lending is recorded
(reporting geography), and what credit costs (cost). The sources differ in date and unit, and all results are descriptive.

## Access
Arhti credit is reported in 31.5% of rural populated mouzas and clusters strongly (Moran's I 0.536). It is not mainly found where
commercial-bank credit is missing: within tehsils, a village that reports bank credit is slightly more likely also to report arhti
credit (odds ratio 1.18, 95% CI 1.09–1.27), although this differs across tehsils (Breslow–Day p < 0.001; 67 tehsils above and 53
below independence). These are reports of what is available, not of what farmers borrow.

## Allocation
Subsistence holdings are 94.1% of farms and receive 91.9% of formal borrower records but 35.9% of the amount lent; holdings above
50 acres are 0.5% of farms and receive 49.4% of the amount. Larger holdings need larger loans, but the gap is wide. Across
districts, small-farm reach ranges from 21.6 to 299.0 borrower records per 1,000 farms under 12.5 acres.

## Reporting geography
Five large-city districts record 39.7% of Punjab's farm-sector disbursement and 59.6% of above-economic disbursement, but hold 13.3%
of cultivated area. Lahore has 190.4 bank branches per 10,000 farms in total but 3.9 rural ones. Districts are as reported by SBP;
whether this is where loans are booked or where the land is remains under verification.

## Cost
Published estimates put the implicit annualized cost of arhti credit at cotton 61.9%; maize 71.48%; rice 80.7%; wheat 67.1%
(IGC 2013, interviews 2012), far above SBP's average agricultural rates of 12.00 to 19.00 percent in FY2012-13. Formal rates were
higher in FY2023-24 (20.67 to 29.42 percent). These are purposive small-sample estimates and institutional averages, not matched
contracts.

## District profiles
Splitting districts at the median of arhti availability and of small-farm reach gives 10 high/high, 10 low/low, 8 low arhti with
high reach and 7 high arhti with low reach; Lahore is not classified. The groups are descriptive, not rankings.

## Conclusion
Finance and farming share Punjab's map only partly. Confirming where SBP attributes loans, and observing how farmers combine arhti,
bank and Kissan Card credit, are the next steps for understanding why.

## Limitations
- The Mouza Census records whether respondents report a credit source as serving the village, not whether households borrow from it.
- Sources differ in date and unit: Mouza Census 2020, SBP FY2023-24 (districts as reported), Agricultural Census 2024 (sample-based), branch list 30 Jun 2024.
- SBP's district attribution is under verification; borrower records may not be unique people.
- SBP's "up to 12.5 acres" and the census's "under 12.5 acres" differ at the boundary; the 36 districts are pre-split units.
- Published evidence on the cost of arhti credit comes from small, non-representative studies.
