# Narrative

## Central question
Why would a farmer pay several times the bank's rate to borrow from a commission agent when the bank is there? We ask whether arhti
credit fills the gaps left by formal credit (H1), whether formal credit reaches small farms in proportion (H2), and whether lending
is recorded where farmland is (H3).

## Main conclusion
The arhti does not begin where the bank ends: the two overlap, and a map of bank branches is not a map of who gets the money. Arhti credit clusters near grain markets and sits alongside formal
credit rather than in its gaps (H1: coexistence, not gap-filling). Formal lending is disproportionate (H2): small holdings are close to
their share of farms in borrower records but receive a much smaller share of the money. Recorded lending is concentrated (H3) in a few
city districts.

## Introduction
One of the authors, supervising banks in Pakistan, kept meeting the same arrangement: a farmer takes fertilizer worth Rs 3,000 from
an arhti, the commission agent who sells his crop, owes Rs 4,000 at harvest, and sells the crop to the same arhti. Few farmers had
worked out the annualized cost, and few minded: their families had borrowed from the same arhti for generations, and he had been
there for every wedding. The bank promised five days, but only once the paperwork was complete, and that could take months. The bank
starts counting time when the documents are ready; the farmer starts counting time when the rain arrives. This account is one
person's professional experience, not a sample; the maps test how far the pattern extends across the province. The app follows four
lenses: where credit is reported as available (access), how formal lending is distributed (allocation), where lending is recorded
(reporting geography), and what credit costs (cost). The sources differ in date and unit, and all results are descriptive.

## Access
- Arhti credit is reported in 31.5% of rural populated mouzas and is strongly clustered (Moran's I 0.536).
- No formal source shows a significant negative association with it. Within tehsils, the odds of reporting arhti credit are higher
  where commercial-bank (1.18), cooperative (1.72) or microfinance (1.76) credit is reported; for ZTBL (0.98) and any formal source
  (0.95) the results are inconclusive.
- The pattern varies across tehsils (Breslow–Day p < 0.001; 67 tehsils above and 53 below independence). These are reports of what
  is available, not of what farmers borrow.

## Market access
Arhti availability is higher where more villages have a grain market within 10 km: one standard deviation more such villages goes
with 5.5 percentage points more arhti availability in the spatial error model (95% CI 0.012 to 0.098; adjusted p 0.039). The link
with cropland does not survive spatial modelling. Exploratory.

## Limited availability
Of 23,130 rural populated mouzas, 3,810 (16.5%) report neither arhti credit nor any examined formal source. This is limited reported
availability of these sources, not financial exclusion: NGOs, rural support programmes and government schemes are not counted.

## Allocation
- In the 36 districts, subsistence holdings are 94.2% of farms and hold 90.9% of borrower records but receive 37.0% of disbursement;
  above-economic holdings are 0.5% of farms and receive 47.8%.
- Disbursement per borrower record is Rs 0.52 million for subsistence holdings and Rs 50.62 million for above-economic holdings.
- Across districts, small-farm reach ranges from 21.6 to 299.0 borrower records per 1,000 farms under 12.5 acres.

## Reporting geography
Five large-city districts record 39.7% of farm-sector disbursement and 59.6% of above-economic disbursement with 13.3% of cultivated
area. The concentration survives leaving out any one city, using the five districts with the largest urban populations instead, and
dropping flagged districts. Districts are as reported by SBP; whether this is where loans are booked or where the land is remains
under verification.

## Cost
Published estimates put the implicit annualized cost of arhti credit at cotton 61.9%; maize 71.48%; rice 80.7%; wheat 67.1%
(IGC 2013, interviews 2012), far above SBP's average agricultural rates of 12.00 to 19.00 percent in FY2012-13. Formal rates were
higher in FY2023-24 (20.67 to 29.42 percent). These are purposive small-sample estimates and institutional averages, not matched
contracts.

What the farmer buys may be more than a loan: credit tied to the sale of the crop, no collateral, repayment that can wait for a
better harvest, a lender who knows him and, for farmers who object to interest, a higher deferred price that they regard as
permissible trade. The same relationship can also tie the farmer to one buyer. These are possibilities from the literature and the
authors' observations, not findings of the maps.

## Kissan Card
Introduced in 2024 after our lending data: an interest-free input loan for holdings of up to 12.5 acres, initially Rs 30,000 per acre
up to Rs 150,000 per season. The initial scheme required applicants to own land with records in the Punjab Land Records Authority
system, which may restrict access for tenants (Government of Punjab, Special Monitoring Unit).

## District profiles
Splitting districts at the median of arhti availability and of small-farm reach gives 10 high/high, 10 low/low, 8 low arhti with
high reach and 7 high arhti with low reach; Lahore is not classified. The groups are descriptive, not rankings.

## Conclusion
Agricultural credit in Punjab runs on two geographies. Informal credit follows grain markets and overlaps formal credit: 79.9% of
arhti-reporting villages also report a formal source (75.9% of other villages do), and no formal source shows a significant negative association. Formal money
concentrates by farm size and by place: small holdings hold 90.9% of borrower records but receive 37.0% of disbursement (36 districts),
and five city districts record 59.6% of above-economic disbursement with 13.3% of cultivated area. The sharpest contrasts in formal
credit are therefore not between places where it is reported and places where it is not, but between small and large holdings, and
between where farms are and where loans are recorded. A farmer who stays with an expensive arhti is not comparing two identical
loans: he is comparing a bank product with a relationship built on trust in place of collateral, flexible repayment and, for many, a
contract regarded as permissible trade rather than interest. The test of financial inclusion is not how many branches a regulator can
count, but whether a farmer has a credible alternative when the arhti sets the terms. Confirming where SBP attributes loans, and
observing how farmers combine arhti, bank and Kissan Card credit, are the next steps.

## Limitations
- The Mouza Census records whether respondents report a credit source as serving the village, not whether households borrow from it.
- Sources differ in date and unit: Mouza Census 2020, SBP FY2023-24 (districts as reported), Agricultural Census 2024 (sample-based), branch list 30 Jun 2024.
- SBP's district attribution is under verification; borrower records may not be unique people; microfinance records have no district.
- The census's class areas below 12.5 acres are internally inconsistent, so area shares are indicative.
- Odds ratios use conventional stratified inference, which does not adjust for spatial dependence among villages.
- Published evidence on the cost of arhti credit comes from small, non-representative studies.
- The opening account is one author's professional observations, not a systematic sample or a Shariah ruling.
