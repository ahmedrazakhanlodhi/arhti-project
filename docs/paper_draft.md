# Where the Bank Ends and the Arhti Begins: Geographic Access, Landholding Structure and the Allocation of Agricultural Credit in Punjab, Pakistan

Ahmed Raza Khan Lodhi and Hasnain Akram, PPHA 38520, University of Chicago. December 2026.

## Abstract

Why would a farmer pay several times the bank's interest rate to borrow from a commission agent? One natural answer is that the
bank is absent. Punjab's credit geography tells a different story. Using the Mouza Census 2020 (23,130 rural populated mouzas in
137 tehsils), State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) lending records for FY2023-24 and the Agricultural Census 2024, we find that arhti
credit is strongly clustered (Moran's I = 0.536), most consistently associated with proximity to grain markets, and reported
alongside formal credit rather than in its absence: within tehsils it is more likely where commercial, cooperative or microfinance
credit is reported. The formal system has a puzzle of its own. Across 36 districts, subsistence holdings are 94.2% of farms and
90.9% of borrower records, yet receive 37.0% of recorded disbursement, and five city districts record 59.6% of above-economic
lending with 13.3% of cultivated area. A map of bank branches is not a map of who gets the money. The findings are descriptive,
area-level associations.

## 1. Introduction: Why pay more when a bank is available?

During his years supervising banks in Pakistan, one of the authors kept meeting the same arrangement. A farmer would take a bag of
fertilizer worth Rs 3,000 from an arhti, the commission agent who markets his harvest, for Rs 4,000 payable at harvest, and then
sell the crop to the same arhti, often at a discount. The price of credit was hidden in two transactions. Few farmers had worked
out the annualized cost, and when he walked them through the arithmetic, they were not particularly troubled. Their fathers and
grandfathers had borrowed from the same arhti families. The arhti had been there when they married off their sons and daughters.
He was, in their telling, like family.

Consider what that relationship buys. For a wedding, the arhti can advance money without collateral, an application or a fixed
repayment schedule. When a flood destroys the crop, he needs no credit officer to establish what happened. The balance can be
carried forward, sometimes for years. The farmer is buying more than fertilizer on credit: he is buying the assurance that a
lender will still be there when things go wrong.

Now consider the bank. Its stated turnaround might be five working days, but the clock starts only when the paperwork is complete,
and in the author's supervisory experience that could take months. Some farmers cultivate inherited land not registered in their
names; others share ownership with relatives or are tenants. Getting the papers in order can mean repeated visits to government
offices and, sometimes, demands for bribes. None of this appears in the quoted interest rate. The bank starts counting time when
the documents are ready. The farmer starts counting time when the rain arrives.

Religion adds another dimension. Farmers who objected to interest-bearing loans often regarded a higher deferred price for
fertilizer as permissible trade, even when its implicit cost was higher. Yet some of them also borrowed from conventional or
Islamic banks for other needs. They were not strangers to formal finance; they were choosing different arrangements for different
purposes.

There is a price to this loyalty. A farmer carried through failed harvests and weddings may not take his produce elsewhere merely
because another buyer offers more. The relationship that protects him can also give the arhti market power. A bank competes by
offering a cheaper loan. The arhti competes by offering a different relationship.

This is not one author's experience alone. Most farmers Haq et al. (2013) interviewed in Punjab in 2012 had bank accounts yet
preferred arhti credit, at an estimated implicit annual cost of 61.9% to 80.7% by crop against bank rates of 9 to 18 percent. Why
does the expensive lender survive beside the cheap one? One natural answer is that banks are missing. Our account suggests
another: banks may be present without offering a convincing substitute.

We take this puzzle to the scale of a province. Does arhti credit concentrate where banks are absent, or where grain markets and
formal credit already are? We compare it with commercial banks, the Zarai Taraqiati Bank (ZTBL), the Punjab Provincial Cooperative
Bank and microfinance banks. A second puzzle lies inside the formal system: small holdings dominate the farm population and the
borrower records, but not the rupees. Before asking how to replace the arhti, we must understand why the farmer has not already
replaced him.

**Thesis.** Agricultural credit in Punjab runs on two overlapping geographies. Arhti credit clusters near grain markets and
alongside formal credit, not in its absence; recorded formal lending is concentrated by holding size and in a few city districts.
Where the banks are is a poor guide to who receives formal money.

We ask two research questions and test three hypotheses. The H1 and H2 tests were fixed in the project log before analysis; H3,
the expanded comparisons and the later market-distance models are exploratory (Table B5 separates planned from post-hoc
regressions).

- **Q1 (tehsil level, geographic exclusion).** Is reported arhti credit availability associated with weaker formal credit
  availability, or does it concentrate where formal credit, grain markets and farmland already are?
  - **H1 (gap-filling).** Arhti credit is reported where formal credit is not: a negative association between arhti and formal
    credit availability across tehsils and, within tehsils, an odds ratio below 1.
  - **H1-alt (coexistence).** Arhti credit is reported alongside formal credit (an odds ratio significantly above 1) and near
    markets and farmland, forming spatial clusters. Estimates indistinguishable from 1 are inconclusive, not evidence for either.
- **Q2 (district level, economic exclusion).** Does formal agricultural credit reach smallholders and tenant-heavy areas in
  proportion to their share of farms?
  - **H2 (proportional reach).** Small holdings' shares of SBP borrower records and disbursement match their shares of farms and
    operated area, and borrower records are spread across districts as small farms are (index of dissimilarity near 0).
  - **H3 (proportional reporting geography, exploratory).** District shares of recorded lending in each holding class match
    district shares of cultivated area (location quotients near 1).

Proportionality is a benchmark, not a prescription: nobody expects a two-acre farm and a hundred-acre farm to need the same loan.
We keep three ideas apart: *presence* (physical banking facilities), *availability* (credit sources reported to serve a village)
and *allocation* (lending as SBP records it). Our datasets differ in date and unit, and we never treat them as contemporaneous.

## 2. Literature and background

**Why formal and informal lenders coexist.** A bank and an arhti face the same basic problem: how to get their money back.
Screening, monitoring and enforcing repayment are costly, particularly when lenders know little about borrowers (Hoff and Stiglitz
1990). One interpretation of high informal interest rates emphasizes the lender's power over a dependent borrower (Bhaduri 1977);
another, the costs of lending where information is scarce. In Chambar, Sindh, Aleem (1990) found an average annual informal
borrowing cost of 78.7 percent against a bank rate of 12 percent, yet informal charges were close to lenders' estimated average
costs, and borrowers were often required to sell through the lender. Bell (1988) reviews these interlinked transactions, in which
finance is bundled with input supply or output marketing. Smith, Stockbridge and Lohano (1999) find that competition among Sindh
traders made interlinked credit behave more competitively than the exploitation view predicts. An expensive loan is not, by
itself, proof of monopoly power; what matters is the borrower's alternatives.

**Arhtis in Punjab.** The arhti combines functions that formal finance often separates. As a licensed commission agent in a grain
market, he brokers crop sales, advances cash or inputs and recovers the advance when the crop is marketed. Haq et al. (2013)
describe arhtis as Punjab's largest source of informal agricultural credit at the time, operating from 325 commodity markets.
Interviews with 18 arhtis in eight locations in 2012 yielded implicit annualized cost estimates, from input-price premiums and
extra commission, of 61.9% for cotton, 71.48% for maize, 80.7% for rice and 67.1% for wheat: crop-specific figures from a
purposive sample, not the price of credit in every village.

**Who formal credit reaches.** Access to an institution does not guarantee access to its money. Malik, Mushtaq and Gill (1989)
document limited institutional credit access among small farm households in Pakistan. Qureshi, Nabi and Faruqee (1996) report that
fewer than a third of rural households borrowed and only about a tenth of their loans came from institutional sources. Khandker
and Faruqee (2003) find that large landowners, about 4 percent of rural households, received 42 percent of formal loans, while
landless and subsistence households, more than 69 percent, received 23 percent. SBP now publishes district lending records by
holding class: subsistence (up to 12.5 acres), economic (above 12.5 to 50 acres) and above-economic (above 50 acres). These data
allow us to examine whether a similar imbalance appears in recorded lending today, although the historical measures are not
directly comparable.

**The geography of finance.** Burgess and Pande (2005) find that India's requirement to open branches in unbanked rural locations
was associated with lower rural poverty. Mian (2006) shows that the distance between a bank's head office and its branches shapes
lending in Pakistan. Leyshon, French and Signoretta (2008) find that British bank branch closures were concentrated in poorer
areas. The question is not whether geography matters, but which geography matters: the location of the branch, the market, the
borrower or the farm.

**Methods literature.** Tobler's (1970) first law, that near things are more related than distant things, motivates testing for
spatial autocorrelation. We use global and local Moran's I (Anselin 1995) and the Getis–Ord Gi* statistic (Getis and Ord 1992; Ord
and Getis 1995), correcting local tests for multiple comparisons with the false discovery rate (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995).
Because our units are administrative, results may depend on the units chosen, the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP; Openshaw
and Taylor 1979; Openshaw 1984), and associations between areas need not hold for the individuals in them, the ecological fallacy
(Robinson 1950). We measure unevenness with the index of dissimilarity (Duncan and Duncan 1955).

**Post-period context.** In 2024 Punjab introduced the CM Kissan Card, an interest-free input loan for holdings of up to 12.5
acres (initial limit Rs 30,000 per acre, capped at Rs 150,000 per season). The initial scheme required applicants to own land with
records in the Punjab Land Records Authority (PLRA) system, which may restrict access for tenants without qualifying ownership
(Government of Punjab, Special Monitoring Unit). Cards became operational on 15 October 2024, after our lending data.

## 3. Data and GIS methods

**Data and units (Table 1).** The Mouza Census 2020 (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, PBS) records, for every village (mouza), which
sources of credit the patwari and other local respondents said serve the mouza's residents. These are reports of availability, not
records of borrowing. We keep Punjab's 23,130 rural populated mouzas and aggregate them to tehsils. From SBP we take FY2023-24
farm-sector borrower records and disbursed amounts by district and holding class; from the Agricultural Census 2024, farms,
operated (farm) area and cultivated area by size class, tenure and crop; from SBP's branch list, all and rural scheduled-bank
branches. Rasters: ESA WorldCover 10 m 2021 (cropland; Zanaga et al. 2022) and the Malaria Atlas Project travel time to cities,
2015 (Weiss et al. 2018).

**Census geography and matching.** Pakistan's census geography nests province → division → district → tehsil → mouza, and PBS
codes each unit hierarchically, much as US FIPS/GEOID codes nest state, county and tract. OCHA's boundaries use their own p-codes.
We matched the 148 census tehsils to OCHA's 139 tehsil polygons by code and name: 115 matched exactly, 11 after rule-based
normalization, 9 after spelling review, 2 as renames, and 8 census units were summed into a parent polygon (attribute join plus
dissolve). The 3 remaining census tehsils, in Lahore, have no rural populated mouzas and no separate polygon. Every non-exact
match was reviewed by hand, and all 23,130 rural populated mouzas were assigned to a polygon. Two of the 139 polygons have no
rural populated mouzas, which leaves 137 tehsils for Q1. The district layer (36 districts, the pre-split units used by SBP and the
census) is a dissolve of the tehsils.

**Projection.** We use an Albers equal-area conic projection centred on Punjab (standard parallels 28°N and 33°N, central meridian
72°E; full definition `+proj=aea +lat_1=28 +lat_2=33 +lat_0=30 +lon_0=72 +datum=WGS84 +units=m`). It is a secant conic, suited to a
mid-latitude region, and equal-area, which matters because choropleths and dot densities compare areas. An earlier choice,
ESRI:102028, is also Albers but centred at 125°E: it rotated Punjab by about 10° and sheared its shapes. A Tissot indicatrix
(Figure S11) shows a maximum angular distortion over the province of 29.68° in ESRI:102028 against 0.11° in our projection.
k-nearest-neighbour weights use geodesic distances, so they do not depend on the projection.

**Normalization and classification.** Every choropleth maps a ratio, never a raw count, because raw counts mostly track the size
of a unit; raw counts appear only as dots and proportional symbols (Figure 6). Class breaks follow a fixed rule: five Fisher–Jenks
classes unless a class would hold fewer than three units, then quantiles. For arhti_share, natural breaks fit best on the goodness
of absolute deviation fit (0.818, against 0.781 for equal intervals and quantiles; Figures S1, S12).

**Raster processing.** Cropland is the fraction of 10 m WorldCover cropland pixels in each 10 × 10 block (about 100 m), an
average-based resampling that avoids the "winner takes all" loss of mixed pixels, summarized by zonal statistics. Travel time to
cities, a cost-distance surface, is a cropland-weighted mean. The Mouza Census reports each mouza's distance to the nearest bank
branch and grain market in bands (in the mouza, 1–10, 11–25, 26–50, over 50 km), which work like multiple-ring buffers.

**Spatial statistics.** Spatial weights are Queen contiguity, row-standardised (primary), with sensitivity runs using Rook
contiguity (which gives the same neighbours for these polygons), geodesic k-nearest neighbours (k = 5; not symmetrized), a
geodesic distance band (73.6 km, the smallest distance that leaves no tehsil without a neighbour, so there are no islands) and
inverse-distance weights within that band. All permutation tests use 999 permutations with seed 20261031 and report one-sided
pseudo p-values in the direction of the observed statistic (the PySAL default, fixed before the analysis; the reference
distribution comes from randomly permuting values across units, so it is not a prespecified directional test). We report global
Moran's I and Geary's C, the Moran scatterplot (Figure S7), local Moran's I (LISA) and Gi* (Queen contiguity including each tehsil
itself, binary weights) at p < 0.05 and after false-discovery-rate correction (Benjamini–Hochberg cutoff 0.0117 for LISA), and a
second-order trend surface, the OLS fit of arhti_share on centroid coordinates x, y, x², xy and y², with its residual map (Figure
S9).

**Arhti and formal credit (H1).** For each formal source (commercial banks, ZTBL, cooperative bank, microfinance banks) we report
the same-tehsil Spearman correlation with arhti_share, the bivariate Moran's I between a tehsil's arhti share and its neighbours'
share for that source, and a within-tehsil Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio of a mouza reporting arhti credit when the formal source is
also reported, with tehsils as strata; we also do this for "any formal source". Tehsils whose 2 × 2 table has an empty row or
column carry no information and are dropped (for commercial banks, 1,456 mouzas). Breslow–Day tests whether the odds ratio is the
same across tehsils, and p-values for the five within-tehsil comparisons are adjusted with Benjamini–Hochberg. Pooled confidence
intervals assume independent mouzas and are not adjusted for spatial dependence. The planned regressions (OLS and a spatial error
model, Table B5) relate arhti_share to commercial and ZTBL availability, distance to an online bank branch, travel time, cropland
and metalled roads. An exploratory model added later replaces roads with distance to grain markets and is fitted by OLS, by a
binomial model on mouza counts with quasi-binomial standard errors, and by a spatial error model (Table B3); Table B9 repeats it
with three market-distance bands (within 10 km, including markets in the mouza; within 25 km; over 50 km), adjusting the spatial
error p-values across the three.

**Allocation and reporting geography (H2, H3).** Province comparisons use the 36 districts on both sides: SBP's 36 district rows
(banks; Islamabad and the 74,494 microfinance borrower records that SBP reports only for Punjab as a whole are excluded) against
the census's Punjab minus the Cholistan Area, which has no SBP counterpart. SBP's All Punjab row (732,856 records) is reported for
comparison. Small-farm reach is subsistence-holding borrower records per 1,000 farms under 12.5 acres, a borrower-record intensity
rather than an inclusion rate, since records may not be unique people. The index of dissimilarity D = ½ Σ |bᵢ/B − fᵢ/F| is the
share of borrower records that would have to be in other districts for their distribution to match that of small farms. The
concentration coefficient orders districts by reach, so it measures inequality in reach across districts relative to small farms,
not inequality between farmers. Location quotients divide a district's share of Punjab's disbursement in a class by its share of
cultivated area. SBP reports lending by district; whether that is the lending branch's district or the financed farm's is being
verified, so every district credit figure is "as reported by SBP". A data-quality screen flags districts where a class's
disbursement per borrower record is above 5 or below 0.2 times the Punjab median, or where above-economic loans exceed 75% of
farm-sector disbursement; the five flagged districts are marked, not dropped (Table B4), and every headline is rerun without them
(Table B8). The five city districts used for H3 (Lahore, Rawalpindi, Multan, Faisalabad, Rahim Yar Khan) were chosen without a
stated rule, so we treat them as an exploratory group and repeat the comparison with a rule-based group, the five districts with
the largest urban populations in the 2023 Population Census (PBS 2023, Table 1; Table B10), and with each city left out in turn.

**Typology.** As an exploratory complement to a median split, we group 35 districts with k-means on six standardized attributes
(arhti and commercial-bank availability, small-farm reach, rural branches per 10,000 farms, tenant share, cropland share), with
and without location, choosing k from 2 to 6 by the silhouette score (Figure S10). Lahore is not classified because it has no
rural populated mouzas, so its availability shares are undefined. All analysis is in Python (GeoPandas, PySAL) and QGIS 4.2.3; the
main maps (Figures 2–6) are QGIS Print Layouts.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 H1: The arhti is not simply a substitute for the bank

Start with the map. Arhti credit is reported in 31.5% of Punjab's rural populated mouzas (Figure 2), but tehsil shares range from
zero to 100% (median 19.2%); 15 tehsils report none. Formal sources are reported in 73.5% of mouzas for ZTBL, 38.2% for commercial
banks, 24.9% for the cooperative bank and 15.2% for microfinance banks. The bank and the arhti leave different footprints.

The pattern is strongly clustered. Global Moran's I is 0.536 (p = 0.001; 0.470 to 0.568 across five weighting specifications),
against 0.297 for commercial-bank credit; Geary's C is 0.458, and the Moran scatterplot places most tehsils in the High-High and
Low-Low quadrants (Figure S7). LISA (Figure 4) finds 25 High-High and 32 Low-Low tehsils at p < 0.05, 15 and 17 after the
false-discovery-rate correction: High-High mainly in Sheikhupura, Gujranwala, Hafizabad, Sialkot and Nankana Sahib, Low-Low mainly
in Rawalpindi, Attock, Jhelum and Chakwal. Gi* finds the same places (28 hot and 33 cold spots; Figure S8). A second-order trend
surface (R² = 0.40) leaves clustered residuals (Moran's I = 0.234, p = 0.002), so clustering persists beyond the fitted
province-wide trend. Arhti availability is also less even than formal availability (index of dissimilarity 0.380, against 0.234
for commercial banks and 0.115 for ZTBL).

**The H1 test (Tables B1, B2).** None of the examined formal sources shows a statistically significant negative association with
reported arhti availability. Across tehsils, arhti_share correlates positively with commercial-bank (ρ = 0.245), cooperative (ρ =
0.391) and microfinance (ρ = 0.380) availability; for ZTBL (ρ = 0.166) and any formal source (ρ = 0.136) the correlations are not
significant after adjustment. The bivariate Moran's I with neighbours' commercial-bank availability is 0.112 (unadjusted p =
0.062; 0.077 adjusted). Within tehsils, the odds that a mouza reports arhti credit are 18% higher where commercial-bank credit is
also reported (odds ratio 1.18, 95% CI 1.09–1.27; 21,674 mouzas in 120 tehsils) and higher still for cooperative (1.72) and
microfinance (1.76) credit, all with adjusted p < 0.001 under conventional stratified inference. For ZTBL (0.98, 95% CI 0.90–1.07)
and any formal source (0.95, 95% CI 0.87–1.04) the results are statistically inconclusive. Scale matters: ignoring tehsils, the
province-wide odds ratio for arhti and any formal source is 1.26, so much of the aggregate association reflects which tehsils have
both sources. The pooled estimates also average over very different tehsils (Breslow–Day p < 0.001 for commercial banks; 67 of 120
tehsils above independence, 53 below).

Of 23,130 rural populated mouzas (Table B2), 5,825 report both arhti and formal credit, 1,465 arhti only, 12,030 formal credit
only and 3,810 neither. Four in five arhti-reporting mouzas (79.9%) report a formal source. But formal availability is already
widespread: 77.2% of all mouzas report a formal source, compared with 75.9% of those not reporting arhti credit. The overlap is
real; it need not imply strong complementarity. Only 6.3% of all mouzas report arhti as their sole examined source. A further
16.5% report neither system, concentrated in the north and west (median tehsil 9.8%; Figure 7B). These are places to investigate,
not evidence of financial exclusion: NGOs, rural support programmes and government schemes are outside this comparison.

**What goes with arhti availability?** Arhti availability is higher where more land is cropland (ρ = 0.49) and lower where more
villages have an online commercial-bank branch, a measure of presence (ρ = −0.40). Market proximity is the more consistent
correlate: arhti availability is higher where more mouzas have a grain market within 10 km (ρ = 0.336; Figure 7A) or 25 km (ρ =
0.377), and both are positively associated in OLS, the quasi-binomial model and the spatial error model before multiple-testing
correction (Table B9). In the spatial error model, one standard deviation more of mouzas within 10 km of a market goes with 5.5
percentage points more arhti availability (95% CI 0.012 to 0.098; p = 0.013, 0.039 adjusted across the three bands); the 25 km
result survives Benjamini–Hochberg (0.042) but not Holm (0.056). Cropland, by contrast, is associated in the non-spatial models
(OLS p = 0.039; quasi-binomial p = 0.021) but not in the spatial error model (−0.026, p = 0.413), which fits far better (λ =
0.715; AIC −22.2 against 34.2). Non-spatial inference is limited: OLS residuals stay clustered (Moran's I = 0.418) and the
binomial model is heavily overdispersed (dispersion 58.9). The planned spatial error model (Table B5) adds two borderline results,
for commercial-bank credit (0.193, p = 0.045) and for mouzas over 25 km from an online bank branch (−0.442, p = 0.036), that do
not hold in the other models; we read them as consistent with arhtis working inside market and banking networks, not as
established effects. **Verdict on H1: the evidence favours coexistence over gap-filling.** No formal source shows a significant
negative association and three show positive ones; the inconclusive results for ZTBL and combined formal availability, and the
local heterogeneity, rule out a universal verdict about substitution.

### 4.2 H2: Counting borrowers is not the same as counting rupees

On the 36-district basis (Figure A1; reconciliation in Table B6), subsistence holdings are 94.2% of farms and account for 90.9% of
farm-sector borrower records but 37.0% of the amount disbursed. Dividing each class's share by its share of farms gives a simple
representation ratio (1 = proportional): 0.97 for subsistence holdings' borrower records and 0.39 for their disbursement; 2.35 and
92.11 for above-economic holdings, which are over-represented even in the count of borrower records. Disbursement per borrower
record is Rs 0.52 million for subsistence holdings and Rs 50.62 million for above-economic holdings, about 97 times as much; this
is not the average loan, since records may count repeat transactions. SBP's All Punjab row, which adds the 74,494 microfinance
records and Islamabad, gives the same picture (91.9% of records and 35.9% of disbursement for subsistence holdings). The census's
class areas are questionable (Table B7): in three classes below 12.5 acres the published area implies an average farm larger than
the class limit (5.38, 9.08 and 15.02 acres), so the area-based benchmark (68.7% of operated area for subsistence holdings) is
provisional and H2 rests on farm counts. The concentration is consistent with earlier evidence of skewed formal credit (Khandker
and Faruqee 2003), although the measures are not directly comparable.

Across districts, the intensity of small-farm borrower records varies widely (Figure 5). Small-farm reach ranges from 21.6 to
299.0 subsistence-holding borrower records per 1,000 farms under 12.5 acres, with a median of 118.5 across 36 districts, and is
spatially clustered (Moran's I 0.532, p = 0.001). The lowest values lie in the north (Rawalpindi, Jhelum, Chakwal, Gujrat); the
highest in the south and centre (Bahawalnagar, Bahawalpur, Okara, Lodhran, Hafizabad). Because the census is sample-based, the
small-farm denominators carry sampling error and rankings of neighbouring districts are uncertain. Figure 6 shows the contrast
without ratios: 4,738 dots, each standing for 1,000 farms under 12.5 acres in the 36 districts and placed only on cropland, show
where small farms are (a synthetic redistribution, not farm locations), and perceptually scaled circles show where small-farm
borrower records are reported. The index of dissimilarity between the two distributions is 0.216, and 0.182 without the five
flagged districts (Table B8). Ordering districts by reach, the concentration coefficient is 0.297 (Figure S6). Small-farm reach is
higher where tenant farms are a larger share of farms (Spearman ρ = 0.39, adjusted p 0.018); this describes districts, not
tenants, whose own borrowing these data cannot show. **Verdict on H2: formal lending is disproportionate.** Small holdings are
close to proportionally represented in borrower records but receive a much smaller share of disbursement, and borrower records are
spread unevenly relative to small farms. Without the flagged districts the subsistence share of disbursement is 39.7%, so the
finding does not depend on them.

### 4.3 H3: Where is agricultural lending recorded?

Where formal agricultural lending is recorded does not follow where farmland is (Figure R1). For above-economic holdings the
location quotient is 14.06 in Lahore and 7.44 in Rawalpindi, and as low as 0.09 in Chakwal. For subsistence holdings it is 4.66 in
Lahore, 6.47 in Jhelum (a flagged district) and 0.29 in Rawalpindi. The five city districts together record 39.7% of Punjab's
farm-sector disbursement and 59.6% of above-economic disbursement, but hold 13.3% of cultivated area (Figure R2): 4.49 times their
area share for large holdings and 1.69 times for subsistence holdings (22.4% of subsistence disbursement). Against farms in the
same class instead of total area, the five record 3.32 times their share of farms of 50 acres or more in above-economic lending,
and 1.22 times their share of small farms in subsistence lending. Lahore and Rawalpindi have little farmland, while Multan and
Rahim Yar Khan are major agricultural districts. The same five districts hold 43.5% of all scheduled-bank branches but 19.1% of
rural branches; the index of dissimilarity against farms is 0.356 for all branches and 0.214 for rural branches.

The pattern does not hinge on the choice of districts or on extreme values (Table B8). Leaving out any one city, the remaining
four still record 4.41 to 5.65 times their area share of above-economic lending (1.33 to 1.97 times for subsistence). The
rule-based group of the five districts with the largest urban populations in the 2023 census (Lahore, Faisalabad, Rawalpindi,
Gujranwala, Multan) records 43.5% of above-economic disbursement with 11.2% of cultivated area. Without the flagged districts,
three of which are city districts (leaving only Multan and Rahim Yar Khan in the group), the above-economic share of all
disbursement falls from 47.8% to 42.0%. **Verdict on H3: recorded lending is concentrated.** The reported concentration is
especially pronounced for above-economic holdings, but is also present for subsistence holdings. Until SBP's district attribution
is confirmed, a high location quotient in a city district may reflect where loans are booked rather than where the financed land
is.

### 4.4 The price of credit and the limits of a district typology

Figure P1 places formal lending rates next to the published arhti estimates, which are not matched contracts. Around 2012-13 the
IGC estimates (61.9% to 80.7% by crop) sat far above both the bank rates farmers reported and SBP's institutional averages for
FY2012-13 (12.00 to 19.00 percent); SBP's FY2023-24 averages are higher (20.67 to 29.42 percent). A median split of districts by
arhti availability and small-farm reach gives groups of 10, 10, 8 and 7 (Lahore unclassified), and k-means separates districts
only weakly (silhouette 0.26 and 0.25; Figure S10).

## 5. Robustness and the MAUP

The clustering of arhti credit holds across five weighting specifications: Moran's I is 0.536 with Queen or Rook contiguity
(identical neighbours), 0.568 with geodesic k-nearest neighbours, 0.470 with the 73.6 km distance band and 0.491 with inverse
distance, all with p = 0.001. **Scale effect:** aggregating tehsils to 35 districts lowers Moran's I to 0.429; 8 High-High and 5
Low-Low districts appear at p < 0.05 but only one of each survives the false-discovery-rate correction (Figure S2), against 15 and
17 at tehsil level. Part of that loss reflects aggregation and part the smaller number of units. **Zoning effect:** excluding the
Cholistan desert mouzas, which changes how three southern polygons are drawn, gives Moran's I 0.543 and a within-village odds
ratio of 1.20 (95% CI 1.11–1.29). Excluding the five flagged districts, the concentration coefficient is 0.255, Moran's I for
small-farm reach is 0.428, and the H2 and H3 headlines change as reported above (Table B8).

## 6. Discussion: The economics of an expensive loan

Three mechanisms are consistent with these patterns, although our data cannot establish them.

*First, the arhti sells a package, not just a loan.* He advances inputs or cash, markets the harvest and recovers the advance from
the proceeds (Bell 1988; Aleem 1990; Haq et al. 2013); a bank must solve the same problems of information and repayment through
separate transactions. The relationship that makes financing easy can also make switching buyers difficult: convenience and market
power can coexist. The market-proximity results fit this account.

*Second, lenders compete on more than price.* Documentation, collateral, delay and the religious acceptability of a contract can
matter as much as the rate (Hoff and Stiglitz 1990; Haq et al. 2013). The village-level overlap of bank and arhti credit makes the
product, not just the branch, the unit of policy analysis, although the census cannot show which households use both (Robinson
1950).

*Third, the banking map can distort the farm map.* If large loans are arranged at city branches, the city receives the statistical
credit even when the farm is far away (compare Mian 2006). A regulator cannot target agricultural finance if it cannot tell the
place of booking from the place of production.

**Policy implications.** The aim need not be to eliminate the arhti because his credit is expensive; it is to give farmers a
credible alternative. Banks could compete on the time from application to disbursement, collateral, Shariah-compliant design,
repayment flexibility and the link between finance and crop sales, not only on rates; warehouse-receipt and value-chain finance
pilots in arhti clusters would test that idea. Competition among traders and lenders may matter as much as the number of outlets
(Smith, Stockbridge and Lohano 1999). The 3,810 mouzas reporting neither source (Figure 7B) are a place to look for unmet demand.
SBP reporting that separates the lending branch from the financed farm would make district targeting possible. And finance built
around land ownership risks leaving tenants behind, as the initial Kissan Card's land-record requirement shows; our FY2023-24 data
are a baseline before it.

## 7. Limitations

The Mouza Census describes reported village-level availability, not household borrowing, contract terms or individual choices. The
datasets differ in time and unit: January–March 2020 for the Mouza Census, FY2023-24 for lending, 30 June 2024 for the branch
list, and 2024 for the sample-based Agricultural Census, whose district figures carry sampling error. SBP's district attribution
is unconfirmed, borrower records need not be unique people, and microfinance records have no district. Some census class areas
imply averages above the class limits, and the census and SBP split holdings differently at exactly 12.5 and 50 acres. We use the
36 pre-split districts. Spatial dependence and overdispersion weaken the independence assumptions of pooled odds ratios; a block
bootstrap or multilevel model would give more cautious intervals. Market-distance bands were added after the first models. Figure
6's dots are a synthetic redistribution, and historical costs come from small, non-representative samples. The opening account
draws on one author's professional observations, not a systematic sample, and is not a Shariah ruling on the contracts described.
All findings are descriptive associations.

## 8. Conclusion

Punjab's maps challenge a simple view of agricultural finance. The arhti is not just the lender of places banks have failed to
reach. His credit is strongly clustered (Moran's I = 0.536; 0.470 to 0.568 across weighting specifications), most consistently
associated with proximity to grain markets, and more likely, within tehsils, where commercial, cooperative or microfinance credit
is reported (H1). Small holdings account for 90.9% of borrower records but 37.0% of disbursement (H2), and five city districts
record 59.6% of above-economic lending with 13.3% of cultivated area, robust to alternative group definitions and exclusion tests
(H3).

Where the bank ends, the arhti has not simply begun: the two often work in the same places. Recall the farmers who knew the arhti
was expensive and stayed with him anyway. They were not comparing two identical loans; they were comparing a financial product
with a relationship that had survived generations, weddings and failed harvests. The central policy mistake would be to confuse
the expansion of a banking network with the expansion of farmers' choices. The test of financial inclusion is not how many
branches a regulator can count, but whether a farmer has a credible alternative when the arhti sets the terms. That is the
question for household research, alongside SBP's loan attribution and an evaluation of the Kissan Card.
